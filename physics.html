Physics Behind the Simulator

The mathematical models implemented in SputterOne — with explanations, equations, and visual diagrams for each.

SputterOne implements a reduced-order physics framework in which each stage of the magnetron sputtering process — from ion-solid interaction through gas-phase transport to film growth — is represented by an established semi-empirical or kinetic-theory equation. This page documents each model, explains the physical meaning of every variable, and provides a visual diagram connecting the mathematics to what you observe in the simulator. Use this page alongside the tutorial chapters and virtual laboratory modules to develop a complete understanding of the sputtering process.

1.

Sputtering Yield

When an energetic Ar+ ion strikes the target surface, it initiates a collision cascade — a sequence of momentum-transferring binary collisions that propagates through the near-surface lattice. The sputtering yield Y is defined as the mean number of target atoms ejected per incident ion, and is the central quantity governing deposition rate and material selectivity. Y depends on three things: the ion energy E (higher energy gives higher yield up to a broad maximum), the surface binding energy U0 of the target material (more tightly bound atoms are harder to eject, so high-U0 materials like W and Ta have low yield), and a threshold energy Eth below which the ion does not have enough energy to eject any atoms at all. In the simulator, Y is computed using the Yamamura-Bohdansky semi-empirical formula, which reproduces experimentally measured yield values within 8-15% across the full ion energy range used in magnetron sputtering.

Y(E) = Q · Sn*(epsilon) · [1 - (Eth/E)^(2/3)]^0.25 · (1 - Eth/E)^2
  • Y = sputtering yield (atoms per ion)
  • E = incident ion energy (eV)
  • Q = material-dependent scaling constant
  • Sn*(epsilon) = universal reduced nuclear stopping cross-section (see Section 2)
  • Eth = threshold energy (eV) — minimum ion energy required for sputtering
  • The term [1-(Eth/E)^(2/3)]^0.25 captures the steep rise just above threshold
  • The term (1-Eth/E)^2 provides the near-threshold cutoff behaviour
What you see in the simulator: The MAX YIELD readout in the Quick Indicators panel shows Y for the current ion energy. Increase the power slider and watch Y increase. Switch from Cu to W and observe how the lower yamQ and higher U0 of W dramatically reduces the yield — and therefore the deposition rate.
2.

Nuclear Stopping — The LSS Function

The nuclear stopping term Sn*(epsilon) quantifies how effectively an ion transfers momentum to target atoms through nuclear (elastic) collisions as opposed to electronic (inelastic) excitations. It is evaluated using the universal Lindhard-Scharff-Schiott (LSS) function, which expresses the stopping cross-section as a function of the dimensionless reduced energy epsilon. The reduced energy epsilon scales the actual ion energy E relative to the characteristic electrostatic interaction energy between the projectile and target nuclei — it accounts for the atomic numbers z1 and z2 and masses M1 and M2 of the projectile and target respectively. At low epsilon (low ion energy or heavy target), nuclear stopping dominates and Sn* is large. At high epsilon the ion moves too fast for efficient nuclear energy transfer and Sn* decreases. For typical magnetron sputtering conditions (Ar+ at 200-800 eV on metals), epsilon falls in the range 2-10 where Sn* is significant but below its peak value.

Sn*(epsilon) = [3.441 · sqrt(epsilon) · ln(epsilon + e)] / [1 + 6.355 · sqrt(epsilon) + epsilon · (6.882 · sqrt(epsilon) - 1.708)]
epsilon = 32.53 · gamma · E / [z1 · z2 · (z1^0.23 + z2^0.23)]
gamma = 4 · M1 · M2 / (M1 + M2)^2
  • epsilon = dimensionless reduced energy
  • gamma = kinematic energy transfer factor
  • z1 = 18 (Ar projectile atomic number)
  • z2 = target atomic number
  • M1 = 39.95 amu (Ar mass)
  • M2 = target atomic mass (amu)
  • e = Euler's number (2.718)
What you see in the simulator: The LSS function is evaluated every frame for each active gun. The YIELD readout reflects both Sn*(epsilon) and the material Q value. Heavy materials like W and Ta have large z2 values which increase the denominator of epsilon, shifting the operating point on the LSS curve.
3.

Angular Emission and Energy Spectrum

Sputtered atoms do not leave the target uniformly in all directions. Their angular distribution follows a cosine-power law — most atoms leave near-normal to the surface, with fewer emitted at oblique angles. The exponent n controls how forward-peaked the distribution is. At low ion energies near threshold, n approaches 1 (pure cosine, Knudsen cosine law). At higher ion energies, n increases above 1 producing an overcosine distribution that is more concentrated around the surface normal. Higher pressure reduces n through gas-phase thermalisation. This angular distribution directly determines the shape of the deposition profile on the substrate — a forward-peaked distribution at low pressure produces a centre-peaked film, while a more diffuse distribution at high pressure produces a broader, more uniform film.

Each sputtered atom is also assigned an initial kinetic energy sampled from the Thompson-Sigmund distribution, which peaks at half the surface binding energy (Es = U0/2) and falls off as Es^-2 at high energies. This energy governs how far the atom can penetrate the background gas before being thermalised.

f(theta) ∝ cos^n(theta)
n = 2.15 + 1.45 * (Eion/500) - fP - fRF - fgeom
n ∈ [0.55, 4.6]
f(Es) ∝ Es / (Es + U0)^3 * [1 - sqrt((Es + U0) / Emax)]
  • theta = polar emission angle from surface normal
  • n = cosine exponent — controls distribution shape (n=1: uniform cosine, n>1: forward-peaked)
  • Eion = ion energy (eV)
  • fP = pressure broadening correction
  • fRF = RF mode spread correction
  • fgeom = geometric correction factor
  • Es = kinetic energy of sputtered atom
  • U0 = surface binding energy (eV)
  • Emax = maximum transferable energy = gamma * Eion - U0
What you see in the simulator: Reduce pressure to 1 mTorr and observe the narrow centre-peaked deposition profile on the substrate — this is the forward-peaked cosine distribution at low n. Increase pressure to 15 mTorr and watch the profile broaden as gas-phase scattering randomises the atom directions.
4.

Gas-Phase Transport

After leaving the target, sputtered atoms must traverse the Ar working gas to reach the substrate. The mean free path lambda is the average distance an atom travels before colliding with a gas molecule. It is derived from kinetic gas theory and is inversely proportional to pressure — at low pressure the gas is sparse and lambda is large (ballistic transport), while at high pressure lambda is small and atoms scatter frequently (diffusive transport).

The scattering ratio chi = d/lambda is the key dimensionless parameter. When chi is much less than 1, atoms travel in nearly straight lines from target to substrate — the flux is highly directional and produces a centre-peaked deposition profile. When chi exceeds 2, the flux is strongly scattered and arrives at the substrate from many directions — producing a broader, more uniform profile but at a lower deposition rate due to flux attenuation.

lambda = kB * T / (sqrt(2) * pi * d_mix^2 * P)
chi = d / lambda (scattering ratio)
Tf = exp(-d / lambda) (transport factor)
Regime classification:
chi < 1Ballistic 1 <= chi < 2Moderate scattering chi >= 2Diffusive
  • lambda = mean free path (m)
  • kB = 1.38e-23 J/K (Boltzmann constant)
  • T = gas temperature (300 K)
  • d_mix = effective molecular diameter of Ar/O2 mixture (m)
  • P = chamber pressure (Pa)
  • chi = scattering ratio (dimensionless)
  • d = target-substrate distance (m)
  • Tf = transport attenuation factor — fraction of flux reaching the substrate
What you see in the simulator: The TRANSPORT RATIO and TRANSPORT REGIME readouts in the Process Diagnostics panel show chi and the regime classification in real time. The Mean Free Path (lambda) value is displayed in the top status bar. Run Lab Module 1 to systematically explore how pressure controls the transition between ballistic and diffusive transport.
5.

Discharge Model

The simulator represents the magnetron plasma discharge through a reduced-order parametric model. The applied target power P and cathode voltage V determine the discharge current I and the effective ion energy at the target surface. In a real magnetron system, the ion energy is governed by the cathode sheath voltage, which depends on the plasma density, magnetic field geometry, and gas pressure. The simulator captures the dominant trends without resolving the full plasma kinetics.

In DC mode, the full cathode voltage accelerates ions across the sheath. In RF mode, an alternating voltage creates a self-bias DC offset that is approximately 60% of the equivalent DC voltage — giving lower effective ion energy and therefore lower sputtering yield at the same nominal power. The magnetic field enhancement factor fB reflects the increased ionisation efficiency of the E×B electron drift trajectory in the magnetron configuration.

I = P / V (discharge current from applied power)
Eion = V * ftype * fB (effective ion energy at target)
DC mode : ftype = 1.06
RF mode : ftype = 0.60 (self-bias reduction)
fB = 0.85 + (B / Bref) * 0.25
Bref = 0.12 T
  • I = discharge current (A)
  • P = applied target power (W)
  • V = cathode voltage (V)
  • Eion = effective ion energy (eV)
  • ftype = DC/RF mode correction factor
  • fB = magnetic confinement enhancement factor
  • B = magnetic field strength (T)
  • Bref = 0.12 T reference field
What you see in the simulator: Switch a gun between DC and RF mode at the same power setting. The YIELD readout will drop by approximately 30% in RF mode due to the lower effective ion energy. The deposition rate drops by approximately 50% because both the yield and the ion flux are reduced. This DC vs RF difference is particularly important when choosing operating mode for reactive sputtering of oxides and nitrides.
6.

Film Uniformity

Film thickness uniformity is one of the most practically important metrics in magnetron sputtering. Non-uniform films produce variation in electrical resistance, optical properties, and mechanical stress across the substrate — which directly affects device yield in semiconductor manufacturing. The non-uniformity metric NU% quantifies the peak-to-peak thickness variation normalised to the mean thickness. A NU% of zero represents a perfectly uniform film; typical production targets are NU% below 3-5%.

In SputterOne, NU% is computed from the binned substrate thickness profile in real time. The deposition profile is shaped by the combination of the cosine emission distribution from the target, the Gaussian spreading kernel, and the pressure-dependent gas-phase scattering. Optimising uniformity requires balancing these factors through target-substrate geometry and pressure selection — which is the subject of Lab Module 3.

NU% = (t_max - t_min) / t_avg * 100
U% = 100 - NU% (uniformity percentage)
where:
t_maxmaximum bin thickness across substrate t_minminimum bin thickness across substrate t_avgmean bin thickness across substrate
  • NU% = non-uniformity percentage
  • U% = uniformity percentage (complement of NU%)
  • t_max = maximum film thickness at any point on substrate
  • t_min = minimum film thickness at any point on substrate
  • t_avg = mean film thickness across substrate
  • Lower NU% = more uniform film
What you see in the simulator: The NU% and UNIFORMITY readouts update in real time in the Process Diagnostics panel. The Radial Thickness Profile chart (PR-303) shows the bin-by-bin thickness distribution visually. Run Lab Module 3 to systematically optimise geometry for best uniformity — the reveal-on-demand panel shows the expected optimal conditions once you have made your own observations.
