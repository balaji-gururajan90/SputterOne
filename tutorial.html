Chapter 01/Foundation
Why
Thin Films?

A thin film is a layer of material ranging from a single atom to several micrometres thick, deposited onto a substrate. Despite their minimal thickness, thin films fundamentally alter a surface's electrical, optical, mechanical, and chemical properties.

Almost every modern technology depends on them - from the copper wiring inside your CPU to the anti-reflection coating on your glasses.

Real-world reference
A 100 nm TiN barrier layer deposited by reactive sputtering is standard practice in CMOS back-end-of-line interconnects. Without it, copper diffuses into silicon within hours at operating temperature and destroys the transistor.
Edelstein et al., IBM J. Res. Dev. 43(3), 1999 — first Cu interconnect integration at 0.25 µm node.
Thickness Ranges
Monolayer (atomic): ~0.2 nm
Semiconductor gates: 1-10 nm
Metal interconnects: 20-500 nm
Hard coatings (TiN): 1-5 um
Decorative / optical: 50-300 nm

  • Semiconductors: Cu/Ta/TiN interconnects in every IC chip
  • Displays: ITO transparent electrodes on every touchscreen
  • Solar cells: CIGS and CdTe absorber layers by sputtering
  • Hard coatings: TiN on drill bits - gold-coloured, 3x harder
  • Optics: Anti-reflection, mirror, and filter coatings
  • Magnetic storage: Co/Pt multilayer films on hard drives

Chapter 02/Pressure & Pumps
Vacuum
Science

Sputtering requires a controlled low-pressure environment. Without vacuum, sputtered atoms collide with air molecules and never reach the substrate. Oxygen and water vapour would also contaminate the film instantly.

At atmosphere (760 Torr), a monolayer of oxygen forms on a clean metal surface in under 1 nanosecond. At 10^-8 Torr base pressure, you have roughly 1000 seconds - enough time to deposit clean film.

Real-world reference
A typical production magnetron sputter tool reaches a base pressure of 10⁻⁸ Torr before introducing working gas. At this pressure the mean free path of residual gas molecules exceeds 5 km — the chamber is effectively empty space.
Ohring, M. — Materials Science of Thin Films, 2nd ed., Academic Press, 2002, Ch. 3.
Vacuum Pump Sequence (Ohring Ch.2)
1. Rotary/scroll pump: atm -> 10^-3 Torr (rough vacuum)
2. Turbomolecular pump: 10^-3 -> 10^-8 Torr (high vacuum)
3. Cryopump: traps residual gas by freezing

Sputtering process: 1-20 mTorr (Ar backfill)
Base pressure required: < 10^-7 Torr

  • Rough vacuum (1-760 Torr): mechanical pumps, food packaging
  • Medium vacuum (10^-3 Torr): fluorescent lamps, incandescent bulbs

  • Ultra-high vacuum (<10^-9 Torr): surface science, particle accelerators
  • Outgassing from chamber walls is the main limit to base pressure - bake-out at 150-300 C drives off adsorbed water

Chapter 03/Gas Selection
Why
Argon?

Not all gases sputter equally. The ideal sputter gas must be chemically inert (won't react with the target or film), and have an atomic mass close to the target to maximise momentum transfer during collisions.

Argon (Ar, Z=18, mass=40 amu) is the perfect balance of cost, availability, inertness, and mass-matching for most metals used in industry.

Real-world reference
Argon is used in over 90% of industrial sputtering processes. Krypton is occasionally substituted for heavy-target materials (W, Ta, Mo) because the closer mass match improves momentum transfer and raises yield by 15–30%.
Depla, D. & Mahieu, S. — Reactive Sputter Deposition, Springer, 2008, Ch. 2.
Momentum Transfer Factor gamma (Simon 4.2)
gamma = 4*m_g*m_t / (m_g + m_t)^2

Ar-Cu: gamma = 4*40*64/(40+64)^2 = 0.944
Ar-Al: gamma = 4*40*27/(40+27)^2 = 0.965
He-Cu: gamma = 4*4*64/(4+64)^2 = 0.222 (too light)

Higher gamma = more energy transferred per collision

  • He (4 amu): too light - poor momentum transfer, low yield
  • Ne (20 amu): better for light targets (Al, Si), expensive
  • Ar (40 amu): optimal for Ti, Cu, Al, Ta - cheap and abundant
  • Kr (84 amu): better for heavy targets (W, Au), but expensive
  • Xe (131 amu): highest yield for heavy metals, very expensive
  • Reactive gases (O2, N2): added to Ar to deposit TiO2, TiN, Al2O3
  • Ar ionisation energy: 15.76 eV - comfortably above typical plasma electron temperatures

Chapter 04/Introduction
What is
Sputtering?

Sputtering is a physical vapor deposition (PVD) process in which atoms are ejected from a solid target material by bombardment with energetic ions - typically Ar+.

The ejected atoms travel through the vacuum and condense on a substrate to nucleate a thin film of the ejected material. The ion energy must exceed the target's surface binding energy (typically 25-40 eV) to initiate ejection.

Real-world reference
The first documented observation of sputtering was by Grove in 1852, who noticed metal deposits forming on glass walls near a platinum cathode in a glow discharge. It took over a century for the Yamamura yield formula to put quantitative predictions on a firm theoretical footing.
Grove, W.R. — Phil. Trans. R. Soc. London 142, 1852. Yamamura & Tawara, ADNDT 62(2), 1996.
Three Energy Regimes (Simon 4.3)
- Subthreshold (<50 eV): yield ~ 0, no sputtering
- Knock-on (50 eV-1 keV): linear cascade, Y = 0.1-3
- Nonlinear cascade (>1 keV): Y = 5-50+, deep implant

Industrial use: regime 2 - predictable, controllable

In this simulator, those models are used in reduced-order form so students can see realistic yield trends in real time without requiring a full atomistic collision simulation.
  • Yield Y = atoms ejected / ion incident - the key efficiency metric
  • Sputtered atoms leave with 3-20 eV energy - much higher than evaporation (~0.1 eV), giving denser, better-adhered films
  • Stoichiometry is preserved - multi-element targets deposit the same ratio
  • Works on metals, alloys, oxides, nitrides - any condensed material

Chapter 05/Hardware
Chamber
Components

A magnetron sputtering system consists of several precisely engineered components. In industrial semiconductor manufacturing, these are arranged in a clustered single-wafer tool - individual process chambers on a central vacuum mainframe.

Real-world reference
In a 300 mm wafer production cluster tool, the substrate never sees atmospheric pressure between deposition steps. The loadlock cycles from atmosphere to 10⁻⁶ Torr in under 60 seconds using a turbomolecular pump backed by a dry scroll pump — no oil anywhere in the system.
Madou, M.J. — Fundamentals of Microfabrication, CRC Press, 2002, Ch. 3.
Clustered Tool Sequence (Simon 4.6.2)
Loadlock -> Degas (150-300 C) -> Preclean
-> PVD Chamber 1 -> PVD Chamber 2 -> Cool

Each wafer processed individually
Never breaks vacuum between steps
Base pressure: 10^-8 - 10^-9 Torr

  • Substrate (anode): wafer or glass held on heated/cooled pedestal
  • Vacuum chamber: stainless steel, cryopumped, base <10^-7 Torr
  • Gas inlet: mass flow controllers for Ar (and reactive gases)

  • Power supply: DC (metals) or RF 13.56 MHz (insulators)
  • Shielding: prevents deposition on chamber walls, must be replaced periodically

Chapter 06/DC / RF / Breakdown
Plasma
Formation

When a high voltage (300-1000 V) is applied across argon gas at low pressure, free electrons accelerate and collide with Ar atoms. Each collision can ionise the atom, producing Ar+ and a new electron - an avalanche ionisation cascade.

DC plasmas work for conductive targets. RF plasmas (13.56 MHz) are needed for insulating targets - the alternating field prevents charge buildup and achieves higher plasma density through longer electron residence time.

Real-world reference
The Paschen curve predicts the breakdown voltage as a function of pressure × electrode gap. For argon the minimum breakdown voltage is approximately 137 V at a pd product of 1 Torr·cm. This is why magnetron systems that compress the plasma magnetically can sustain discharge at much lower pressures than a simple diode configuration.
Lieberman & Lichtenberg — Principles of Plasma Discharges, Wiley, 2005, Ch. 2.
Ionisation Reaction
e- + Ar -> Ar+ + 2e- (threshold: 15.76 eV)

Plasma density: 10^9-10^11 cm^-3
Electron temperature: 1-5 eV
DC diode: ~1-5 mA/cm^2
Magnetron: ~10-100 mA/cm^2

  • Sheath (cathode fall): thin electron-depleted layer at cathode - most voltage drop occurs here, accelerating Ar+ into the target
  • Secondary electrons: ejected from target by ion impact, re-enter plasma to sustain ionisation (5-10% yield per ion)
  • RF at 13.56 MHz: electrons oscillate in field, increasing collision probability -> denser plasma at lower pressure
  • Bias sputtering: RF bias on substrate allows simultaneous deposition and re-sputtering to improve film density

Chapter 07/ExB Drift
Magnetron
Design

A magnetron uses permanent magnets behind the target to create a magnetic field parallel to the target surface. The crossed electric field (E, normal to target) and magnetic field (B, parallel) produce an ExB drift that traps secondary electrons in cycloidal orbits above the target.

This confinement dramatically increases ionisation efficiency - electrons make multiple orbits before being lost, ionising far more Ar atoms per electron than a simple diode discharge.

Real-world reference
A standard unbalanced magnetron of the type used in most research sputter tools produces an E×B electron drift that follows the racetrack erosion groove visible on a used target. The racetrack on a 3-inch Cu target after 50 kWh of operation is typically 1–3 mm deep and causes the target to be scrapped at roughly 30% material utilization.
Kelly & Arnell — Vacuum 56(3), 2000 — review of magnetron sputtering.
ExB Drift Velocity
v_drift = |E x B| / B^2

B field typical: 0.01-0.05 T
Electron confinement: 5x more orbits vs diode
Plasma current: 10-100x higher than diode
Racetrack erosion: visible groove on target surface

  • Magnets must be motor-driven in industrial tools - static magnets cause non-uniform target erosion (racetrack groove) and uneven film deposition
  • Full-face erosion is required - redeposited material on uneroded zones flakes off as particles, contaminating the film
  • Typical magnet configurations: cardioid, figure-8, or proprietary orbital paths
  • ExB drift has direct analogies to cyclotron motion and the Hall effect

Chapter 08/Yamamura / Bohdansky
Sputtering
Yield

The sputtering yield (Y) defines how many target atoms are ejected per incident ion. It depends on the ion energy, the masses of the ion and target, and the target's surface binding energy.

Real-world reference
Published experimental yields for 500 eV Ar⁺ on Cu are in the range 2.0–2.5 atoms/ion, depending on surface cleanliness and crystal orientation. The Yamamura formula predicts approximately 2.3 at this energy — agreement within 10–15% is typical across the material set.
Yamamura & Tawara — Atomic Data and Nuclear Data Tables 62(2), 1996.
Yamamura / Bohdansky Reduced-Order Yield Fit
Y(E) ~ Q x S_n(E) / U0 x (1 - (E_th/E)^(2/3))^0.25

Reduced-order simulator form:
Y(E) follows a Yamamura/Bohdansky-style energy fit with threshold, binding-energy, and bounded angle corrections.

U0 = surface binding energy (eV)
E_th = threshold energy
Q = material-dependent empirical coefficient
Cosine Emission Law
j(theta) = Y x f x cos(theta) / p

Sputtered atoms leave in cosine distribution
Low energy: undercosine (flatter)
High energy: overcosine (forward-peaked)

Chapter 09/Gas Transport
Mean Free
Path

After ejection, sputtered atoms must travel through the argon gas to reach the substrate. The mean free path (lambda) is the average distance an atom travels before colliding with a gas molecule.

If the target-substrate (T-S) distance is much greater than lambda, atoms scatter randomly and the film becomes non-directional, rough, and non-uniform.

Real-world reference
At 5 mTorr Ar and 300 K, the mean free path is approximately 10 mm. A typical research sputter tool has a target-to-substrate distance of 50–100 mm, placing the process in the moderate-to-high scattering regime. This is why substrate rotation is standard — it averages out the non-uniform angular flux.
Thornton, J.A. — J. Vac. Sci. Technol. 11(4), 1974 — zone model of sputtered film structure.
Kinetic Theory Formula (Ohring Ch.2)
lambda = kT / (sqrt(2) x pi x d^2 x P)

k = 1.38x10^-23 J/K | T = temperature [K]
d = collision diameter ~3.7x10^-10 m (Ar)
P = gas pressure [Pa]

In this simulator, those models are used in reduced-order form so students can see realistic yield trends in real time without requiring a full atomistic collision simulation.
  • At 1 mTorr (0.13 Pa): lambda ~ 5 cm - directional, low-scatter film
  • At 20 mTorr (2.7 Pa): lambda ~ 0.3 cm - heavy scatter, diffuse film
  • Optimum T-S distance: 1-3x the mean free path
  • High pressure gives better step coverage (conformal) but lower deposition rate
  • Low pressure gives directional deposition, better for deep features (interconnects)

Chapter 10/Deposition
Film
Uniformity

Film thickness uniformity across the substrate is critical for device yield. A non-uniform film means different resistance, optical properties, and mechanical stress across the wafer - leading to die-to-die variation and yield loss.

Real-world reference
Semiconductor fabs specify film thickness uniformity at ±1–2% across a 300 mm wafer. Achieving this with a 3-inch target requires substrate rotation, carefully optimised target-to-substrate distance, and sometimes a physical shadow mask to trim the edge flux.
Rossnagel, S.M. — J. Vac. Sci. Technol. A 21(5), 2003 — review of film thickness uniformity.
Non-Uniformity Metric
NU% = (t_max - t_min) / (2 x t_avg) x 100%

Target for IC manufacturing: NU% < 5%
High-end processes: NU% < 1%

Improved by: substrate rotation, T-S distance,
target size, collimation

Chapter 11/TiN / Hysteresis
Reactive
Sputtering

Instead of sputtering a compound target, reactive sputtering deposits a pure metal target (Ti, Al, Ta) and flows a reactive gas (N2, O2) into the chamber. The sputtered metal atoms react at the substrate to form the desired compound film.

This gives precise stoichiometry control - adjusting the reactive gas flow directly changes the film composition. TiN is the classic example: gold-coloured, extremely hard, used as a diffusion barrier in every modern CPU.

Real-world reference
The classic hysteresis experiment by Berg et al. on Ti in Ar/N2 shows that as N2 flow increases the deposition rate stays near the metallic value until a critical flow, then drops abruptly by 60–80% as the target transitions to TiN mode. Recovering the metallic rate requires reducing N2 well below that critical point — the loop does not retrace itself.
Berg et al. — J. Appl. Phys. 63(3), 1988 — hysteresis in reactive sputtering.
Hysteresis Behaviour (Simon 4.5)
Low N2 flow -> metallic mode: high rate, low V
At transition point A -> target poisons abruptly
High N2 flow -> reactive mode: low rate, high V

Reducing N2 does NOT immediately recover -
must sputter-clean the target surface first
This history-dependence = hysteresis

Chapter 12/Multi-Gun
Co-
Sputtering

Co-sputtering uses multiple targets simultaneously - each bombarded by independent guns. Atoms from different materials mix during transport and co-deposit, forming alloy thin films with tunable composition.

By adjusting individual gun power, you control the atomic ratio in the film - enabling the deposition of complex multi-element alloys, oxides, and nitrides.

Real-world reference
Indium tin oxide (ITO) — the transparent conductor in most flat-panel displays and touch screens — is deposited by co-sputtering In₂O₃ and SnO₂ targets in an Ar/O₂ atmosphere. The 90:10 In:Sn composition that gives optimal conductivity and transparency is controlled by the relative power settings of the two guns.
Minami, T. — Semicond. Sci. Technol. 20(4), 2005 — transparent conducting oxides.
Composition Control
Film at% ~ (Y1 x P1) / Sum(Yi x Pi) x 100%

Y = yield | P = power per gun
This simulator supports 3 independent guns:
Cu + Al + Zn (or any combination)

Chapter 13/FM / VW / SK
Film Growth
Modes

When sputtered atoms land on the substrate, they don't all behave the same way. Depending on surface energy, temperature, and lattice mismatch, three distinct growth modes emerge - each producing a fundamentally different film morphology.

Real-world reference
STM images of Fe deposited on Au(111) at room temperature show classic Volmer-Weber island growth — isolated 3D clusters form and only coalesce into a continuous film above 5–8 monolayers. The same Fe on an Fe(100) substrate grows layer-by-layer (Frank-van der Merwe) because the lattice mismatch is zero.
Bauer, E. — Z. Kristallogr. 110, 1958 — original classification of epitaxial growth modes.
Controlling Parameters (Ohring Ch.5)
Surface mobility ~ Temperature
Critical thickness ~ 1 / Lattice mismatch

High T + low mismatch -> Frank-van der Merwe
Low T + low mismatch -> Volmer-Weber
Any T + high mismatch (>7%) -> Stranski-Krastanov

  • FM - epitaxial semiconductor layers: Si/Ge, GaAs on GaAs

Three Growth Modes
01 - FRANK-VAN DER MERWE - LAYER BY LAYER
High mobility - fills entire surface before next layer. Atomically flat, epitaxial.
02 - VOLMER-WEBER - ISLAND GROWTH
Atoms prefer bonding to each other. Random 3D islands nucleate. Rough, granular films.
03 - STRANSKI-KRASTANOV - LAYER THEN ISLANDS
Flat wetting layers first, then strain drives island nucleation. Quantum dots (InAs/GaAs).
Chapter 14/Metrology
Film
Characterisation

After deposition, the film must be measured and verified. Thickness, resistivity, uniformity, and composition are all critical parameters that determine whether a film meets specification.

Real-world reference
A 100 nm Cu film deposited at 5 mTorr with a deposition rate of 1 nm/s can be fully characterised in under two hours: XRR gives thickness and density, XRD gives grain size and texture, four-point probe gives sheet resistance, and a profilometer step scan gives thickness at the film edge. These four measurements together define the process window.
Birkholz, M. — Thin Film Analysis by X-Ray Scattering, Wiley-VCH, 2006.
Four-Point Probe (Simon 4.7)
R_sheet = 4.532 x V / I [ohm/square]

Outer probes: current source
Inner probes: voltage measurement
Thickness = rho_bulk / R_sheet
Probe spacing: 0.5-2.0 mm typical
